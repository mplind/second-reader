The running machine, or draisine, built by Karl von Drais in 1817, was the first
steerable two-wheeler; the rider pushed it along with the feet. Pedal-driven
velocipedes followed in the 1860s, with cranks fixed to the front wheel. Because a
fixed crank ties speed to wheel size, makers enlarged the driven wheel, producing the
high-wheeled ordinary of the 1870s.

The decisive change was the rear-wheel chain drive. John Kemp Starley's Rover of 1885
put the rider low between two similar-sized wheels and drove the rear wheel through a
geared chain, and the pattern became known as the safety bicycle. John Boyd Dunlop's
pneumatic tire of 1888 completed the modern form. Chain-driven safeties spread rapidly
through the late 1880s, and catalogues from the mid-1890s show them almost entirely
displacing the ordinary.
